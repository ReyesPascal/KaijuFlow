# Restraint makes a results report beautiful

A results page becomes beautiful and effortless to read mostly by subtraction: the research, the design systems and the best real pages all converge on a calm, conventional, verdict-first page built from a very small kit. People judge a page's looks within about 50 milliseconds, that judgement is driven far more by clutter and by whether the page looks like what they expect than by colour, and it carries over into how usable and trustworthy the page feels ([Lindgaard et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290500330448); [Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf); [Tuch et al. 2012](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/38315.pdf)). Readers then spend **57% of their viewing time on the first screenful** and mostly take in headings, first words and numbers ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). The reusable kit is short: one column with the verdict at top left; body text of 16 to 19px in lines of about 66 characters; one type family in two weights; a grey scale plus one accent colour, with status always carried by a word and a shape as well as a colour; bars that start at zero, are labelled directly and are grey except the one that matters; tables with right-aligned, equal-width digits and hairlines in place of boxes; and headings that state the finding in plain words. For Speed Check that means replacing the dashboard mosaic with a short single-column report (a verdict sentence, three or four headline numbers each beside its target, six identical target rows, one four-bar comparison, and a before-and-after table where the dumbbell chart was), plus a Letter page in the same order that never relies on background colour to carry meaning. The rules and numbers below were read at their sources unless flagged; the specific values proposed for Speed Check are the researchers' synthesis of those rules, not something any single source prescribes, and a few areas (Tufte's own books, The Economist, Material 3 layout numbers, real one-page print exemplars, award-jury reasoning) were never verified.

## Looks are judged in 50 milliseconds, and clutter is what loses

Three bodies of evidence explain why a results page should be calm before it is anything else. The first is perception research. Ratings of a page's appeal after 50 milliseconds agree closely with ratings after half a second ([Lindgaard et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290500330448)), and that first judgement leaks into everything after it: in a cash-machine experiment, how good the interface looked changed how usable people rated it after using it, "whereas the degree of actual usability had no such effect" ([Tractinsky, Katz and Ikar 2000](https://www.ise.bgu.ac.il/faculty/noam/papers/00_nt_ask_di_iwc.pdf)). What drives the judgement is measurable. Across 450 websites rated by 548 volunteers, models of visual complexity and colourfulness explained about half the variation in appeal; **high visual complexity caused the largest drop in appeal, colourfulness played "only a minor role"**, and readers over 45 preferred low complexity more than any other age group ([Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)). Complexity in that model is essentially a count of distinct regions on the screen. A second study adds prototypicality, meaning how much a page looks like others of its kind: pages with low complexity and high prototypicality were rated most appealing within 50 milliseconds, and unfamiliar-looking pages were "generally judged as being unattractive" whatever their complexity ([Tuch et al. 2012](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/38315.pdf)). A questionnaire validated across seven studies splits website beauty into four facets, simplicity, diversity, colourfulness and craftsmanship ([Moshagen and Thielsch 2010](https://www.uni-muenster.de/OWMS/uploads/drafts_thielsch/pdf/moshagen_2010.pdf)). Simplicity (order, grouping, balance) and craftsmanship (ideas "implemented with skill and care") are the two a plain report can raise without adding anything.

The second body of evidence is how people read. In NN/g's original study 79% of users scanned a new page and 16% read word by word; rewriting a page to be concise, scannable and neutral in tone raised measured usability by 58%, 47% and 27% respectively, and by 124% combined ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)). Users have time to read at most 28% of the words on an average visit ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)), see roughly the first two words of a heading or list item ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-2-words-a-signal-for-scanning/)), and scan best when clear headings let them skip to the part they want, the "layer-cake" pattern ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)). Attention is also top-heavy: 57% of viewing time goes to the first screenful, **74% to the first two**, and within the first screenful more than 65% goes to its top half ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). The UK's statistics office reports the same from its own research: "80% of users only read the main points in a publication" ([ONS](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/content/writing-for-users/writing-main-points-and-analysis)).

The third is the difference between a dashboard and a report. Stephen Few defines a dashboard as a display "consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance" ([Few, Dashboard Confusion](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/ie/dashboard_confusion.pdf)), and in a later paper says "the key word is 'monitor'" ([Few, Dashboard Design](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)). A one-off result with a ranked conclusion and a fixed reading order is not monitoring, and tile grids work against it: cards de-emphasise ranking and are "a poor choice when users need to compare between multiple options" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/cards-component/)). The shape that fits is a single column that reads verdict, evidence, detail, which is also how GOV.UK tells designers to start every page ([GOV.UK](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/styles/layout/)).

This evidence has limits. The appeal studies rated general websites, not short reports with charts; the researchers found no study of reading order on a one-page report that mixes text and charts; Lindgaard's paper was read as an abstract only; and NN/g's 79% figure dates from 1997. The direction of the findings has held for decades, but the exact percentages belong to their original settings.

## The reusable rules, with their numbers and sources

These rules hold for any results page and, with the density change described in the next section, for product screens. Where a number is the researchers' own synthesis the text says "inferred".

### Layout: one column, one left edge, space before boxes

**Put the verdict at top left of a single column and keep everything else quiet enough for it to stand out.** Few calls the upper left "the prominent position of the screen" and spending it on a logo or navigation "a waste of prime real estate"; logos and navigation "aren't data and should therefore be subdued" ([Few, Common Pitfalls](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)). He also explains why restraint is a precondition for emphasis: "When everything is visually prominent, nothing stands out", and items "can only be effectively highlighted if you begin with a fairly neutral and balanced display" ([Few, Common Pitfalls](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf); [Few, Dashboard Design](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)). Stretching a section "to fill up the space" is one of his named faults.

**Group with space first and add a box only when space fails.** Items close together read as one group, and proximity "can overpower competing visual cues such as similarity of color or shape" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/gestalt-proximity/)). A border or background groups more strongly still, but borders added "in an abundance of caution" give "busy, cluttered designs", "using whitespace alone to create clear groupings reduces the visual complexity of a design", and full-width contrasting bands create "false floors" where people stop scrolling ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/common-region/)). The US government design system turns this into numbers: use "less whitespace to group elements and more whitespace to distinguish them", put at least **1.5 times as much space above a heading as below it**, at least 1em between paragraphs and 0.5em between list items ([USWDS](https://designsystem.digital.gov/components/typography/)). Atlassian uses 0 to 8px inside compact components, 12 to 24px inside larger ones and 32 to 80px between page-level blocks ([Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/foundations/spacing)). Alignment is itself a grouping cue: "people assume that aligned items are related to each other" and read indented items as subordinate ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout)), and Carbon asks for "visible key lines" on which several objects align ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/2x-grid/overview/)). The researchers' working rule (inferred) is that the gap inside a group should be no more than half the gap between groups, with one shared left edge for headings, text, first table columns and labels.

**Use a small fixed spacing scale inside a contained page width.** The four systems below agree closely. The researchers' synthesis for a report (inferred, but inside all four) is a scale of 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48 and 64px, side padding of 16px on phones and 32px from about 1024px, and a container of 1020 to 1056px with running text held to about 66 characters. Carbon states the principle behind the numbers: "Sections of a UI are allowed to be dense, but the whole page should not be crowded" ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/spacing/overview/)).

**Limit hierarchy to about three sizes and three levels of contrast.** NN/g's rule is "Use no more than 3 sizes" (14 to 16px body, 18 to 22px subheads, up to 32px headers), no more than three contrast variations and at most two big elements; its squint test is to blur the page by 5 to 10px and check that the grouping and emphasis still read ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/)). Greying text until it is hard to read is not a hierarchy tool: reducing contrast to de-emphasise "is dangerous" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/principles-visual-design/)). Cards suit mixed, clickable summaries; they are "less scannable than lists", and a drop shadow signals that a block can be clicked, so shadows on static blocks send a false signal ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/cards-component/)). Detail on demand is good practice one level deep; designs with more than two disclosure levels "typically have low usability" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)), and each control needs a hit area of at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)).

**Design the phone width first.** Content must reflow without sideways scrolling at a width of 320 CSS pixels ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow.html)), and Atlassian tells designers to "always include mobile" at 320 to 479px ([Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/foundations/grid)). Only about two wordy columns fit legibly on a phone (numeric tables fit more), rotating the phone is "a last resort", and a table that must scroll sideways should lock its first column and show a cut-off column as the cue ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-tables/)).

### Typography: larger text, a 66-character line, two weights

**Choose a familiar, well-made typeface and spend the effort on size, line length and spacing.** In the largest font study (352 readers, 16 fonts) people read **35% faster in their fastest font than their slowest** (314 against 232 words a minute), but "there was not a single font that increased reading speed for everyone", and preference did not predict speed: readers were fastest in the font they preferred only 20% of the time, which is chance with five fonts ([Wallace et al. 2022](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/Readability_TOCHI22.pdf)). "Looks good" and "reads fast" therefore have to be checked separately, with the actual reader. Apparent size depends on x-height (the height of the lowercase letters), not the nominal size: Verdana's is about 55% of the type size, Baskerville's about 40% ([Google Fonts Knowledge](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/using_type/the_complications_of_typographic_size)). A quick legibility check for any sans is whether capital I, lowercase l and the numeral 1 can be told apart ([Google Fonts Knowledge](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/readability_and_accessibility/introducing_accessibility_in_typography)).

**Set body text larger than habit suggests, in lines of about 66 characters.** In an eye-tracking study of 104 readers, comprehension was best at 18 and 26 point, and the authors recommend that "text-heavy websites should use fonts of size 18 or larger"; they tested only Arial on a low-resolution monitor, so the result is a direction, not a precise figure ([Rello et al. 2016](https://pielot.org/pubs/Rello2016-Fontsize.pdf)). The same study found line spacing mattered little except at the extremes, which hurt. On line length, Mary Dyson's research review corrects a common simplification: on screen, reading *speed* rises with longer lines, "surprisingly even up to 100 cpl", but comprehension is poorer and "moderate line lengths (around 50–70 cpl) are preferred" ([Dyson](https://legible-typography.com/en/6-overview-of-research-typography)). A report meant to be understood belongs in that band; the CSS `max-width: 66ch` lands on the USWDS target (the CSS is inferred).

**Build hierarchy from few sizes, two weights and space.** Butterick's advice is to emphasise a heading "by putting space above and below", to use bold not italic, and to ignore mathematical scales: "When your headings look right, they are right. The ratio is irrelevant" ([Butterick](https://practicaltypography.com/headings.html)). The scales the major systems ship show how few steps are needed. Two details in them are worth copying: Material 3's largest roles are regular weight, so size alone carries the top of the hierarchy, and Carbon's small headings are simply the body size at weight 600 ([Material tokens](https://github.com/material-components/material-web/blob/main/tokens/versions/latest/sass/_md-sys-typescale.scss); [Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/typography/type-sets/)). For emphasis inside text the weight jump must be obvious: "Avoid using semibold for emphasis in regular text. Use bold or extrabold" ([Google Fonts Knowledge](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/choosing_type/exploring_typefaces_with_multiple_weights_or_grades)). Apple advises against light weights for small text ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography)); the real pages measured use very light weights only at large sizes.

**Space capitals out, tighten very large type, and leave body text alone.** Capitals slow continuous reading by 5 to 10%, which Microsoft's Kevin Larson attributes to practice, not to lost word shapes ([Larson](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/develop/word-recognition)); so capitals are for labels of a few words, and GOV.UK dropped them from its status tags because uppercase "can be harder to read" ([GOV.UK](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/tag/)). Tightening lowercase letter-spacing "makes reading more difficult" and "the defaults determined by type designers are appropriate" ([Dyson](https://legible-typography.com/en/6-overview-of-research-typography)). Large display type is tightened slightly: the pages measured for this research apply −1.12px at 56px (Stripe), −1.2px at 80px (Apple) and −4.32px at 72px (Vercel's Geist) ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/annual-updates/2024); [Apple](https://www.apple.com/environment/); [Geist](https://vercel.com/geist/typography)). Text is set flush left; centred and justified text are discouraged ([USWDS](https://designsystem.digital.gov/components/typography/)).

**Use equal-width digits wherever numbers stack, and normal digits in sentences.** "Tabular figures are essential for one purpose: vertically aligned columns", while proportional figures suit body text, and lining figures (all the same height) are the right choice beside capitals ([Butterick](https://practicaltypography.com/alternate-figures.html)). Tabular lining figures also suit charts and any number that changes but must stay aligned ([Google Fonts Knowledge](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/introducing_type/understanding_numerals)). In CSS this is `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums lining-nums` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/font-variant-numeric)). Between a number and its unit, NIST requires a space ("30.2 °C", and it applies the rule to % as well) ([NIST SP 811](https://www.nist.gov/pml/special-publication-811/nist-guide-si-chapter-7-rules-and-style-conventions-expressing-values)); Google's style guide specifies a non-breaking space so the unit never wraps away ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/units-of-measurement)); the UK statistics office alone drops the space for one-letter units ([ONS](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/content/numbers/measurements-and-units)).

**Add a second typeface only for a reason.** Google's first question is "Do we really need a secondary typeface?", and it accepts showing "data alongside editorial text" as a reason; when in doubt, "pair a serif and a sans serif" that share proportions ([Google Fonts Knowledge](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/choosing_type/pairing_typefaces)). The candidates below are all on Google Fonts with the axes shown ([Google Fonts metadata](https://fonts.google.com/metadata/fonts)). That catalogue does not record tabular figures, which were confirmed at the maker's site only for Inter.

**Let the font adapt to size and theme.** An optical-size axis lets one family serve captions and giant numerals: small sizes get "less stroke contrast, larger x-heights, wider characters, and more open spacing", browsers apply it automatically, and it typically doubles the font file ([Google Fonts Knowledge](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/choosing_type/choosing_typefaces_that_have_optical_sizes)). Light text on a dark background appears to glow and thicken ("halation"), and Google suggests choosing weights per theme so both "feel the same", ideally through a grade axis, which thickens strokes without changing widths ([Google Fonts Knowledge](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/choosing_type/exploring_typefaces_with_multiple_weights_or_grades)). Of the families checked only Roboto Flex has a grade axis ([Google Fonts metadata](https://fonts.google.com/metadata/fonts)), and neither Material nor Apple prescribes a weight change for dark surfaces; both manage dark text through colour.

**Print is smaller and tighter.** Body text is 10 to 12pt with line spacing still 120 to 145%: "Nearly every book, newspaper, and magazine is set smaller than 12 point" ([Butterick](https://practicaltypography.com/point-size.html)). One-inch margins on Letter paper give a 6.5-inch line, too long for body text; at 12pt, margins of 1.5 to 2.0 inches "will usually give you a comfortable line length", though the real target is characters per line ([Butterick](https://practicaltypography.com/page-margins.html)). GOV.UK is the only design system found publishing print values: its body prints at 14pt on a 1.15 line ([govuk-frontend](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-frontend/blob/main/packages/govuk-frontend/src/govuk/settings/_typography-responsive.scss)). White type on a printed colour fills in with ink and needs a heavier weight ([Google Fonts Knowledge](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/choosing_type/exploring_typefaces_with_multiple_weights_or_grades)).

### Colour: a grey scale, one accent, and status that survives greyscale

**Build the palette as one grey scale plus one accent, and name colours by their job.** Every system examined works this way. Carbon: "The neutral gray family is dominant … Additional colors are used sparingly and purposefully" ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/color/overview/)). Material 3, Apple, GitHub's Primer and Atlassian all separate raw colours from role names (background, surface, border, text, accent) whose meaning stays fixed while their values change per theme ([Material 3](https://m3.material.io/styles/color/roles); [Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color); [Primer](https://primer.style/product/getting-started/foundations/color-usage/); [Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/foundations/color)). Radix publishes the most directly reusable recipe: twelve steps, each with a job. A grey tinted toward the accent hue (Radix's "natural pairing", for example slate with blue or indigo) gives "a more colorful and harmonius vibe"; pure grey is safer when several status hues sit on it ([Radix](https://www.radix-ui.com/colors/docs/palette-composition/composing-a-palette)). None of the editorial or report pages measured uses pure black for text: Our World in Data uses navy, the FT a warm dark grey, Apple rgb(29,29,31) ([OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/life-expectancy); [FT](https://ig.ft.com/); [Apple](https://www.apple.com/environment/)).

**Use colour only to say something.** Few's rules are the clearest statement: "Use color only when needed to serve a particular communication goal", "use different colors only when they correspond to differences of meaning in the data", and "use soft, natural colors to display most information and bright and/or dark colors to highlight information that requires greater attention" ([Few 2008](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/rules_for_using_color.pdf)). Apple says to reserve colour "for elements that truly benefit from emphasis" and never to let one colour mean two things ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/color)). Datawrapper calls grey "the most important color in Data Vis" because it makes the highlight stand out ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/colors)). The research says restraint costs nothing: colourfulness played only a minor role in appeal, and high colourfulness was penalised by some groups ([Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)). The popular "60-30-10" proportion has no primary source that the researchers could find and should not be cited as a rule.

**Never let colour carry status alone.** The accessibility standard is explicit: "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information" ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)); two colours that also differ in lightness by a contrast ratio of 3:1 or more count as having a second cue ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). Carbon builds a status indicator from symbol, shape, colour and text and says "at least three of these elements must be present"; a change such as faster or slower "must have either a '+' or '-' sign, a caret, or an arrow icon", after which colour is optional ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/patterns/status-indicator-pattern/)). GOV.UK moved its tags from white text on a dark fill to dark text on a pale fill because users mistook the dark ones for buttons, and it names tags with adjectives, not verbs ([GOV.UK](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/tag/)). The UK Government Analysis Function says accessible red, amber and green shades do not exist, so a status cell must be filled *and* contain the status word ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-colours-in-charts/)). In practice PageSpeed Insights pairs green with a circle, amber with a square and red with a triangle ([PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/analysis?url=https%3A%2F%2Fweb.dev%2F)), and its report stylesheet keeps a bright colour for fills and a darker one for text (pass `#0c6` and `#080`, average `#fa3` and `#C33300`, fail `#f33` and `#c00`) because the bright ones are too light to read as text ([Lighthouse stylesheet](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse/blob/main/report/assets/styles.css), origin of the saved copy presumed).

**Choose hues that survive colour-blindness.** Colour-vision deficiency affects up to 8% of men ([Nuñez et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0199239)). Few's rule is to "avoid using a combination of red and green in the same display"; blue with red is his substitute ([Few 2008](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/rules_for_using_color.pdf)). Okabe and Ito's eight-colour set was built for this: blue `#0072B2`, vermilion `#D55E00` in place of red, bluish green `#009E73` in place of green, orange `#E69F00`, sky blue `#56B4E9`, yellow `#F0E442`, reddish purple `#CC79A7` and black; thin lines and small marks need the darker blue and orange, not sky blue and yellow ([Okabe and Ito](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/)). Paul Tol's "high-contrast" trio (blue `#004488`, yellow `#DDAA33`, red `#BB5566`) is the only categorical set he documents as also working "in a monochrome printout" ([Tol](https://sronpersonalpages.nl/~pault/data/colourschemes.pdf)). The Analysis Function's pair for two series is dark blue `#12436D` with orange `#F46A25`, and its "one thing highlighted" palette is `#12436D` against grey `#BFBFBF` ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-colours-in-charts/)).

**For magnitude, step one hue evenly from light to dark.** Few: "stick with a single hue … and vary intensity from pale colors for low values to increasingly darker and brighter colors for high values" ([Few 2008](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/rules_for_using_color.pdf)). The Analysis Function allows five steps at most (`#092135`, `#12436D`, `#2073BC`, `#6BACE6`, `#ADD1F1`) and requires the numbers as well, because the ramp fails contrast rules by itself ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-colours-in-charts/)). Carbon reverses the ramp in dark themes ("the lightest color denotes the largest values") and forbids a continuous gradient in place of stepped classes ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/)). Even steps should be measured in a perceptual colour space: sRGB blue and yellow share an HSL lightness of 50% but are 0.452 and 0.968 in OKLCH, which is why CSS now offers `oklch()` ([CSS Color 4](https://www.w3.org/TR/css-color-4/)). Tailwind publishes its whole palette that way, with a neutral ladder at 98.5, 97, 92.2, 87, 70.8, 55.6, 43.9, 37.1, 26.9, 20.5 and 14.5% lightness ([Tailwind](https://tailwindcss.com/docs/colors)). Two-sided (diverging) schemes "do not work" after greyscale conversion ([Tol](https://sronpersonalpages.nl/~pault/data/colourschemes.pdf)).

**Meet these contrast numbers.** The ratios are WCAG 2.2's; the 7:1 aim comes from Apple and Google as well.

| What | Minimum contrast | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Body text | 4.5:1; aim for 7:1, especially for small text in custom colours | [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/), [Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/dark-mode), [Google Fonts Knowledge](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/readability_and_accessibility/introducing_accessibility_in_typography) |
| Large text (24px and up, or 18.5px bold) | 3:1; 4.5:1 at the stricter level | [W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html) |
| Chart marks, status glyphs, anything needed to understand a graphic | 3:1 against adjacent colours | [W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html) |
| Chart marks whose values are also printed as labels | Exempt | [W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html) |
| Gridlines and decorative separators | No requirement | [W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html), [Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-colours-in-charts/) |
| White body text on the base dark surface | 15.8:1, so text still passes 4.5:1 on the lightest raised surface | [Material 2](https://m2.material.io/design/color/dark-theme.html) |

Two shortcuts follow from the systems' own scales. In Carbon's ten-grade palette, text needs to be grade 60 or darker on white for 4.5:1 ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/color/overview/)). The only grey Tol found that reaches 4.5:1 on white is `#757575`, and W3C's own 3:1 example grey is `#949494` ([Tol](https://sronpersonalpages.nl/~pault/data/colourschemes.pdf); [W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). W3C also warns that thin fonts and thin lines render fainter than their nominal colour, so elegant light type needs contrast above the minimum ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)).

**Rebuild colours for dark mode; do not invert them.** Apple: dark palettes "aren't necessarily inversions of their light counterparts" ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/dark-mode)). Material 2 recommends a dark grey surface, `#121212`, not black; sets text at 87% white for high emphasis, 60% for medium and 38% for disabled; and warns that saturated colours "produce optical vibrations against a dark background", recommending lighter, desaturated tones ([Material 2](https://m2.material.io/design/color/dark-theme.html)). Raised or grouped areas get lighter, never darker: Carbon steps from `#161616` to `#262626` and on, one step per layer ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/color/overview/)). Radix keeps the solid accent identical in both themes (blue `#0090ff`, green `#30a46c`, red `#e5484d`) and rebuilds the tints and text shades around it ([Radix](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/radix-ui/colors/main/src/dark.ts)). Red text on a dark background looks nearly black to some colour-blind readers ([Okabe and Ito](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/)). The theme should follow the system setting, because people "expect all apps and games to respect their preference" ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/dark-mode)).

**On paper, only lightness and ink survive.** Browsers print in `economy` mode by default: they may "leave out all background images" and adjust text colours. Setting `print-color-adjust: exact` asks them not to, but "there isn't any guarantee that print-color-adjust will do anything", because the user's print settings win ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/print-color-adjust)). Bright greens "are not printable" and come out dull and dark ([Okabe and Ito](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/)). The Analysis Function's test is to check every chart in greyscale print preview ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-colours-in-charts/)).

### Charts and tables: bars from zero, direct labels, hairlines

**Title the chart with the finding and explain the measure underneath.** The Analysis Function gives every chart "a headline title and a formal statistical subtitle", where the headline "should communicate the headline message of the chart"; its test is to imagine "describing what the chart is showing over the phone" ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/)). The Urban Institute says "Use active, concise titles", left-aligned, with the unit stated once ([Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)). Our World in Data uses a variant that suits non-experts: a short plain title with one grey sentence beneath that defines the measure ([OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/life-expectancy)). Published text sizes follow.

| Guide | Title | Subtitle | Axis and data labels | Source and notes | Chart size |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BBC ([bbplot](https://github.com/bbc/bbplot/blob/master/R/bbc_style.R)) | 28pt bold, `#222222` | 22pt | 18pt | 16pt | 640 by 450px |
| Urban Institute, web ([guide](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)) | 20px | 16px | 12px (legend 14px) | 11px | 760px wide |
| Urban Institute, print | 12pt | 10pt | 8.5pt (legend 9.5pt) | 8pt | 6.25in wide |
| Datawrapper ([text](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/text-in-data-visualizations)) | Biggest, boldest, highest contrast | | Over 12px; two levels, "like a 12px gray and a 14px black" | Small, thin, grey | |

**Label directly and delete the legend.** BBC: "it's better to label data directly with text annotations" ([BBC](https://bbc.github.io/rcookbook/)). Urban: "When possible, avoid using legends and instead directly label the data" ([Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)). The Analysis Function gives the reason: legends rely on matching colours, which fails readers who cannot see them; it also keeps all chart text horizontal and at least size 12 ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/)). Datawrapper adds that units belong next to the numbers and that a label should say what the number is: "3.4% unemployed", not "3.4%" ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/text-in-data-visualizations)).

**Draw bars from zero, sorted, with thin gaps, in grey plus one highlight.** Bars must start at zero because their length is the value ([Few](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Communicating_Numbers.pdf); [FT Visual Vocabulary](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/main/visual-vocabulary/README.md)). "The width of the bars should be about twice the width of the space between the bars" ([Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)). "Always rank bars by value, unless there is a natural order", never break the axis, and where a target exists "you should always include it" ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/)). Bars run horizontally when labels are long, and paired bars "become tricky to read with more than 2 series" ([FT Visual Vocabulary](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/main/visual-vocabulary/README.md)). On colour: "If all the bars measure the same variable, make them all the same color", and start with grey, colouring only what should be noticed ([Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)). Length and position are judged accurately; area and angle, as in pies, donuts and gauges, are judged poorly ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)).

**Strip the furniture.** With value labels at the bar ends, "Urban style is to omit y-axis labels and gridlines" ([Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)). Otherwise use at most ten light gridlines (`#D9D9D9` "is fine"; the BBC uses `#cbcbcb`), axis lines and labels in a grey such as `#595959`, and no shaded backgrounds, borders, 3D or shadows ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/); [Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-colours-in-charts/); [bbplot](https://github.com/bbc/bbplot/blob/master/R/bbc_style.R)). The BBC adds one heavier baseline at zero in `#333333` ([BBC](https://bbc.github.io/rcookbook/)). Few's summary rule: parts that are not data should be shown "just visibly enough to perform their role, but no more so" ([Few 2008](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/rules_for_using_color.pdf)).

**Match the form to the comparison.** For before and after, the Urban Institute warns that "if the order of the dots differs between groups, readers can find dot plots difficult to navigate", suggests an arrow to show direction, and prefers separate charts or a normalised measure such as percent change to a shared or dual axis ([Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)). The Analysis Function says to use a table when readers should compare values, when percentages are involved, or when "you need to show values of very different sizes in the same display" ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-tables/)). For a value against a limit, Few's bullet graph is the specified form: a dark bar "approximately 1/3rd the thickness of its container", the limit as "a short line that runs perpendicular to the orientation of the graph", and two to five background bands in shades of one hue (two bands at 35% and 10% black; three at 40%, 25% and 10%), with the shading order reversed when lower is better ([Few](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/misc/Bullet_Graph_Design_Spec.pdf)). PageSpeed Insights uses a close cousin, a thin three-zone bar with a pin where the value falls ([PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/analysis?url=https%3A%2F%2Fweb.dev%2F)). For a short history, Tufte's sparkline is "a small intense, simple, word-sized graphic" with the normal range shown "as a gray band" ([Tufte](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparkline-theory-and-practice-edward-tufte/)), and Urban marks the first, last, highest or lowest point ([Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)). GitHub's Primer supports bar, line and area charts and does not support creating new donut charts or sparklines ([Primer](https://primer.style/product/ui-patterns/data-visualization/)), which is a reason to treat both as optional extras.

**In tables, align, thin the rules and never box.** Richard Rutter: "Left-align text, right-align numbers, and align headings with data"; use the same number of decimals and tabular lining numerals; put no border around the table; use rules "in one direction only" and light; do not stretch a table to full width; group rows with space ([Rutter](https://alistapart.com/article/web-typography-tables/)). The Analysis Function puts numbers to be compared in columns, marks only "the line under the header row and the lines above and below any summary statistics", states units in the column heading and ranks rows by value ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-tables/)). Urban uses a 1pt rule under the column headings and under the last row, optional dotted light-grey row rules (RGB 217, 217, 217), and an em dash for missing values; it also allows a small bar beside each number ([Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)). Carbon's screen sizes are rows of 32, 40 or 48px, 14px text, a 14px header at weight 600 and 16px side padding ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/data-table/style/)). Zebra stripes are optional: a study of 244 people found "no evidence that zebra striping consistently improves the accuracy or speed", and a timed follow-up found it helped on three of eight questions and never hurt ([Enders](https://alistapart.com/article/zebrastripingdoesithelp/); [Enders](https://alistapart.com/article/zebrastripingmoredataforthecase/)).

**Coloured cells need the number inside them.** Use at most five steps of one hue, keep 3:1 contrast between cell fill and text, and sort the rows ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-colours-in-charts/); [Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-tables/); [Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)). An outlier gets a note in words ("Annotate it"), not a log scale, which "many readers will not understand" ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/text-in-data-visualizations); [Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)).

**Give every headline number a comparison and no spare precision.** Few: a bare number "means little. Compared to what? Is this good or bad?"; add a target or some history and "a quick visual means" of judging good or bad, and never show more precision than the reader needs ("$3,848,305.93 rather than $3,848,306 or perhaps even $3.8M") ([Few, Common Pitfalls](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)). Urban notes that "a single, large number" can replace a chart ([Urban Institute](https://urbaninstitute.github.io/graphics-styleguide/)). GOV.UK's one rule for a statistic headline is that "the statistic and description must make sense when read aloud" ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/statistic-headlines), address not confirmed by the researchers).

**Give each chart a text equivalent.** W3C asks for a short description that identifies the chart and a long one giving "scales, values, relationships and trends", ideally shown to everyone as part of the page ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/)). The Analysis Function places it directly under the chart, says it should not "repeat the chart title, be a literal description or outline every data point", and prefers SVG with real text to an image ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/)).

### Wording: the verdict first, the finding in the heading

**Lead with the conclusion and make headings state findings.** "Put the most important information first" ([GOV.UK](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-structure/)); readers then "get the main point, regardless of how much they read" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/)). The US plain-language guide ranks heading types: question headings first, statement headings "the next best choice because they are still very specific, but bold and direct", and topic headings such as "General" last ([Digital.gov](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design/headings)). Because readers see about the first two words, the informative words go first ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-2-words-a-signal-for-scanning/)), and headings take sentence case ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/headings)). The measured pages do exactly this: Our World in Data heads sections with findings ("Life expectancy has increased across the world") and Apple writes its figures into sentences ("A water reuse rate of 43%.") ([OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/life-expectancy); [Apple](https://www.apple.com/environment/)).

**Keep sentences to 15 to 20 words and paragraphs to one idea.** "Aim for 15 to 20 words per sentence and one concept per paragraph. Don't overload sentences with lots of numbers" ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/writing-about-statistics-2/)); GOV.UK's ceiling is 25 words ([GOV.UK](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2014/08/04/sentence-length-why-25-words-is-our-limit/)). Paragraphs run to "no more than 150 words in three to eight sentences", never over 250, and each opens with a topic sentence ([Digital.gov](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/clear-short)). Use the active voice ([GOV.UK](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)) and a neutral tone: objective wording measured 27% better than promotional wording ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)). NN/g's target for a broad audience is an 8th-grade reading level ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/legibility-readability-comprehension/)).

**Write numbers so they can be compared and trusted.** Use numerals, not words ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-writing-show-numbers-as-numerals/)). Keep the same number of decimal places across a set and round to "the lowest level possible for the intended user" ([ONS](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/content/numbers/rounding)), because "figures with lots of detail give an impression of high accuracy that may be unwarranted" ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/writing-about-statistics-2/)). State a change with its level and its comparison point, as in "9.0 million overseas visits in August 2022, up 31% from the previous month" ([ONS](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/content/writing-for-users/writing-main-points-and-analysis)). W3C's guidance for readers with cognitive disabilities asks for content that is "usable without understanding math concepts" and notes that percentages are easily confused ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)), so every number should carry its verdict in words.

**Use common words, explain a term where it first appears, and name things by what they are.** Specialist terms are allowed: "You just need to explain what they mean the first time you use them" ([GOV.UK](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)). The simplest method is "adding a simple language term in brackets next to it" ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)). A glossary is a backup: "Signpost users to a glossary, but don't force them to rely on one. Do not place glossaries at the start" ([Analysis Function](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/writing-about-statistics-2/)). Hover tips must not hold vital information and do not exist on touchscreens or paper ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tooltip-guidelines/)). For names, "pick words that describe what the feature does or represents" ([Shopify Polaris, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20241214012144/https://polaris.shopify.com/content/naming)), keep one term for one thing ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing)), and avoid branded terms in labels ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ui-copy/)). Status labels are adjectives ([GOV.UK](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/tag/)).

## The best real pages use one small kit at two densities

The two strongest speed-result pages are opposites that agree on order. Netflix's fast.com is one sentence and one numeral: "Your Internet speed is" at 40.5px bold, the number at 315px in regular weight, the unit at 81px beside it, near-black on white, six text styles in total, no boxes, and everything else behind a single "Show more info" button ([fast.com](https://fast.com/)). Google's PageSpeed Insights opens with a one-line verdict, "Core Web Vitals Assessment: Failed", with only the outcome word coloured; then repeats one unit per metric (shape icon, name, value coloured by state, three-zone bar with a pin); separates cells with 1px hairlines, not boxes; collapses passed checks behind "PASSED AUDITS (10)"; and confines the run conditions to a quiet grey strip at the foot ([PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/analysis?url=https%3A%2F%2Fweb.dev%2F)). Cloudflare's speed test adds a plain-language block that scores "Streaming", "Gaming" and "Video Calls", translating raw numbers into everyday outcomes ([Cloudflare](https://speed.cloudflare.com/)).

The admired product dashboards share a kit so small it can be listed in a sentence: one sans family, 13 to 14px body, 12px labels in grey or capitals, a neutral surface, one accent hue, hairlines in place of boxes, and tables whose rows carry a pale bar proportional to the value. Plausible puts six figures in one row split by vertical hairlines (12px uppercase label, 19.2px value at weight 600, change in grey with a small coloured arrow) and draws one indigo line with faint gridlines and no legend ([Plausible](https://plausible.io/plausible.io)). Fathom sets its headline figures at 48px in a very light weight inside the page's only dark band ([Fathom](https://app.usefathom.com/demo)). Linear uses three greys for text on near-black, 17px body on a 27.2px line, 1px borders and its accent on one element only ([Linear](https://linear.app/insights)). Vercel's Geist states the logic: "Copy 14" is the "most commonly used text style", and "Tabular is used when conveying numbers for consistent spacing" ([Geist](https://vercel.com/geist/typography)).

Editorial and report pages use the same restraint at a larger size. Body text runs 16 to 20px on a line 1.4 to 1.55 times its size, in a column of roughly 460 to 800px: Our World in Data sets 18px on 27.9px in a 604px column, Stripe 18px on 28px in 460px, The Pudding 20px on 28px in 800px ([OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/life-expectancy); [Stripe](https://stripe.com/annual-updates/2024); [The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/2024/11/love-songs/)). Editorial sites pair a serif for titles with a sans for text and data (Playfair Display with Lato at Our World in Data, Financier with Metric at the FT), keep titles moderate (about 40px) and never heavy, while corporate reports use one sans at large sizes (Apple's opening line is 80px at weight 600) ([OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/life-expectancy); [FT](https://ig.ft.com/); [Apple](https://www.apple.com/environment/)). Designed reports often sit on tinted paper (cool off-white rgb(246,249,252) at Stripe, warm rgb(245,243,238) at the Gates Foundation's Goalkeepers), where reference pages use white ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/annual-updates/2024); [Goalkeepers](https://www.gatesfoundation.org/goalkeepers/report/2025-report/)). Apple's device of a short phrase in the text colour followed by a sentence in grey at the same size is a ready-made form for a verdict and its reason ([Apple](https://www.apple.com/environment/)).

The difference in density is the one real fork, and purpose resolves it. A report read once by one person belongs with the editorial pages and with Carbon's "expressive" type set (16px base, made for web pages); a screen used daily belongs with the dashboards and Carbon's "productive" set (14px base, fixed headings, for pages with "a higher density of information") ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/typography/type-sets/)). So for KaijuFlow's own screens the rules above stay and three things change: body drops to 13 to 14px with 12px labels; cards become legitimate for mixed, clickable summaries; and Few's single-screen rule applies wherever a screen is truly for monitoring. Interactive parts add their own numbers: 24 by 24px targets, stronger borders on controls (Radix steps 7 and 8), and a dark theme that follows the system setting.

From these pages the researchers drew three style directions, which are their synthesis and not any maker's claim. "One Number" (fast.com, Stripe) is a poster: a verdict sentence, one giant numeral, almost nothing else. "Calm Instrument" (Plausible, Fathom, Linear) is a refined dashboard: a hairline-divided band of figures and row-bar tables. "Scorecard" (PageSpeed Insights) is a verdict line followed by identical metric units against thresholds. A fourth, "editorial" flavour (serif title, larger text, tinted paper) rests on thinner evidence. The blend that every observed page supports is the poster's opening, the scorecard's target rows and the instrument's tables, set at editorial text sizes.

## Speed Check becomes a single-column report with the verdict on top

Everything in this section is the researchers' application of the rules above, so all of it counts as inferred. It is built from the content the research notes describe: a verdict; six targets where lower is better; a few headline numbers; a comparison of four setups; the change since the last run across four measures with mixed units; a ten-title by four-setup table with one outlier; a short run history; three findings; a next step; and a note on method and terms, in about 400 to 700 words. The earlier dense dashboard was the wrong pattern for this content, which has a fixed reading order and a ranked conclusion that tile grids hide.

### The web page

The first screenful should hold the verdict, the headline numbers and the start of the target rows, with the verdict in its top half, since that is where most reading time goes. The researchers' reading of Few's single-screen rule for a one-off report is that only things compared together, the numbers and their targets, need to share a screen.

For wording, the verdict gives the count first and then the miss, for example "The new setup meets 5 of 6 targets. Cold start missed its limit: 4.8 seconds against 4.0." (the figures are placeholders taken from the notes). A change gives direction, amount and both values: "Cold start is 0.6 seconds faster than last run (5.4 to 4.8)". Status words are "Met" and "Not met", and "Faster", "Slower" and "No clear difference"; the last matters because Google's own guidance says run-to-run scores vary with "changes in underlying conditions" and are better thought of as a distribution ([Lighthouse](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/lighthouse/performance/performance-scoring)). A house rule that satisfies every unit source is "seconds" spelled out in prose and headings, and the unit once in a table's column heading with bare numbers below. One decimal place follows the rounding rules, provided the differences between setups survive it. Terms are explained in place, for example "cold start (the first request after a restart)". The four setups take parallel, descriptive names that lead with the distinguishing words, such as "New setup (KaijuFlow + AltMount)" and "Today's setup (Usenet Ultimate + NzbDAV)"; for the third, the notes' strongest candidates are "AltMount without KaijuFlow" and "AltMount direct". "Add-on" is the wrong word by Microsoft's definition, which reserves it for hardware and uses "add-in" for software that extends a program ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/a-z-word-list-term-collections/a/add-in-add-on)). No source tests names for compared setups.

## What is verified, what is inferred, and what was never checked

**Inferred.** Every Speed Check specification above is synthesis: the type roles and sizes, the spacing scale, the page widths, the mapping of Radix steps to report roles, the dark-theme hex set, the print order and sizes, the wording patterns and the setup names. So are the working rules stated as such (inside gap no more than half the between-group gap; `max-width: 66ch`; a first screenful of roughly 1080px on desktop) and the three style directions. Setting the unit smaller than the numeral has no primary source; it is observed at fast.com and is common practice. Clipping an outlier's bar is the researchers' suggestion.

**Flagged as unverified in the notes.** The often-quoted claim that 55 characters per line is optimal (attributed to Dyson and Haselgrove 2001) was not found; 55 was simply the middle of three lengths tested. Tabular figures were not confirmed for any family except Inter. Material 3's layout numbers (window size classes, 4 and 8dp increments) were never loaded. Material 3's baseline hex values, Apple's system grey values, Primer's neutral values and Carbon's full grey scale could not be extracted. The meaning of ColorBrewer's print and photocopy flags was read from memory, so "keep a printed ramp to three or four classes" is unverified. APCA, a newer contrast method, is described by its author as "still in development" as a candidate for WCAG 3, and its standing in the draft itself was not checked ([APCA](https://github.com/Myndex/SAPC-APCA/blob/master/documentation/APCAeasyIntro.md)). What browsers print by default, per browser, was not checked, nor was support for `@page` margin boxes or the `letter` keyword. No primary source was found for a minimum print type size, for an official reading-level target, or for the "60-30-10" rule. Several addresses were inferred, not confirmed: Few's whitepapers, Larson's paper, the Braille Institute page, GOV.UK's statistic-headlines page, some Digital.gov and ONS pages, and the origin of the Lighthouse stylesheet.

**Thin or second-hand evidence.** Lindgaard, Kurosu and Palmer and Schloss were read as abstracts only. The Gestalt principles rest on NN/g's summaries, not the original papers. All "observed" page measurements are computed styles read on 14 November 2026 at one window size; in the second pass the screenshots were never viewed, the FT and Reuters were seen as index pages only, Stripe, Octoverse and Goalkeepers as landing pages only, and Cloudflare's test only in its empty state. No designer's own account of why a page looks as it does was read, apart from Vercel's typography page. Okabe and Ito's colour values were read by eye from a 2002 image and converted to hex by the researchers. The finding that readers prefer value labels at bar ends comes from an informal poll. Rello's size study used one font on an old monitor.

**Sources that disagree.** NIST and Google put a space before a unit; ONS drops it for one-letter units. NN/g says to use numerals even at the start of a sentence; ONS says a sentence should not start with one. USWDS asks for at least 1em between paragraphs; the strictest WCAG level asks for 1.5 times the line spacing, and the notes could not tell whether the two are meant to coexist. Rutter says avoid zebra striping; Enders found it never hurt. Datawrapper's contrast figures (2.5 and 4) are lower than WCAG's. Few puts colour-blindness at 10% of men where the other sources say about 8%. Material 2's text opacities were confirmed in one research pass and not found in another.

**Never researched.** Tufte's own statements of data-ink and chartjunk (only his sparkline page was read); The Economist's chart rules; the NYT Upshot and Bloomberg Graphics; any real one-page printed exemplar such as a fund fact sheet, energy report or lab report; year-in-review pages; what award juries praise; Shopify Polaris's chart guidance; any design system's specification for a headline-number tile; any experiment comparing dumbbell charts or bullet graphs with bars for general readers; and any experiment comparing a glossary with in-place definitions.

## Conclusion

The strongest finding is the convergence itself. Perception studies, government style guides, newsroom chart rules and commercial product pages were gathered separately and for different reasons, yet they reduce to the same two instructions: remove what is not the message, then align what is left. No single number in this report carries as much weight as that agreement, which is why the unverified items, real as they are, do not threaten the direction.

Two implications follow for how to work. First, the printed page is the best test of the screen page: a design that still reads in greyscale, with backgrounds stripped and no hover, has by construction put its meaning in words, shapes and position, which is exactly what the accessibility and colour-blindness rules demand. Designing the Letter page early will improve the web page. Second, the report and KaijuFlow's future screens can share one set of role-named colour, type and spacing variables and differ only in density, so the effort spent on Speed Check is the first instalment of the product's design system. What the sources cannot settle is taste and this one reader's speed, since preference does not predict either; the remaining uncertainty is cheaper to resolve with a ten-minute read of a draft than with more literature.
